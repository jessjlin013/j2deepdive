Deep Dive Prepared for J2 Ventures · November 2026

The future of medical physical AI is gated by simulation and validation, not proof of concept

Current trends in AI optimization of analytics and workflow will quickly evolve to physical processes in the next decade. Robotic systems have proven to effectively run and refine laboratory procedures, and they will progress to assist clinicians in critical settings. I see the primary bottleneck in simulation training and validation, and that is where J2's dual-use thesis complements the battlefield care data needed to establish trust in autonomous medical care. My thesis points to Inner Logic, a J2 portfolio company, and a handful of companies that J2 can consider.

01 · Why now

Proof of concept is no longer the barrier, and validation has the early tailwinds to invest in and scale

Robots have now carried out surgical steps on their own, autonomous devices are reaching the FDA, and the government is paying for autonomous medtech directly. What is missing is the layer that proves these systems are safe across thousands of patients: simulation and data.

Cleared

Proof of concept

  • A Johns Hopkins robot trained on surgery videos performed a gallbladder-removal phase on a realistic patient with 100% accuracy.
  • The FDA has cleared Parallel Robotics' handheld needle driver and Revolve Surgical's table-mounted robot (March 2026), and granted AutonomUS's AI-guided vascular-access device Breakthrough designation.
  • Inner Logic's team trained orthopedic trauma AI entirely in simulation, then ran it on real hardware.
Funded

Government demand

  • ARPA-H: up to $175.3M over five years for autonomous stroke intervention (AIR).
  • DARPA: about $32.4M for autonomous systems that stop internal bleeding (MASH), plus a $3.5M surgical robotics competition.
  • Defense Health Agency: funded AI-GUIDE from idea to startup in three years.
The gap

Validation at scale

  • There is no accepted way yet to show an autonomous system is safe across the full range of patients, anatomy and failure cases.
  • That is the work simulation and data infrastructure does, and it is where the least capital has gone.

Why the simulation and data layer is the gap

Coverage

Physical testing can't reach the edge cases

Cadaver and animal labs are expensive and cover a narrow range of anatomy. Autonomy has to be tested against variations in anatomy, imaging and failure scenarios that are hard to reproduce physically. Only simulation can generate them at scale.

Data

Operating-room data is mostly uncaptured

Surgical video is rarely turned into structured data. Uncovr, one of the first companies trying, analyzed thousands of hours across 400+ operating rooms and found missed billable steps in 16% of procedures, a sign of how little of the OR is recorded accurately today.

Rules

The standard for simulation evidence is new

The FDA issued final guidance on computational modeling in November 2023. In MDIC's most recent industry survey (2022, about 40 device makers), 61% named uncertain regulatory expectations as the biggest barrier, and a 2025 review of 72 studies still lists regulatory fragmentation and limited data access as the main obstacles.

Capital

Money has gone to robots and robot brains

Investors have funded general-purpose robot AI at large scale, while medical validation startups are still at seed or Series A. Even ARPA-H is paying to build its own simulation testbed for the robots it funds.

03 · Why medtech is hard

Three problems a warehouse robot never faces

Medicine is one of the most valuable places for physical AI, and one of the hardest. Fewer than 6% of eligible procedures are done robotically today, so the room to grow is large, but each of these barriers slows adoption.

Data

You can't collect a million surgeries

Robots learn from repetition. Every patient's anatomy is different, and failure cases are rare and can't be staged on people. Training data has to come from video, simulation and small numbers of real cases.

Evidence

Proof is slow and expensive

Devices are tested in cadaver and animal labs that are costly and only cover a narrow range of anatomy. An autonomous system multiplies the cases a regulator will want to see.

Trust

Someone has to be accountable

Surgeons, hospitals and the FDA all need to know who is responsible when an autonomous step goes wrong. That is why DARPA frames its program as building a partner for surgeons, not a replacement.

Why defense pulls first

In large-scale combat or mass-casualty events there are not enough surgeons, and the wounded may be hours from one. DARPA's surgical competition is explicitly trying to create surgical capacity on demand, and MASH targets internal bleeding, a leading cause of survivable death on the battlefield. The military will accept autonomy sooner because the alternative is often no care at all. Civilian hospitals follow once the evidence exists.

Bull case
  • Picks and shovels. Inner Logic sells to every device maker building autonomy, so it doesn't have to pick which robot wins.
  • A rare team. A surgeon, a simulation researcher and a surgical-autonomy pioneer, with a sim-to-real result already shown.
  • Regulatory tailwind. FDA guidance from 2023 and 2025 sets out how computational modeling can count as evidence in device submissions.
  • Dual-use fit. Trauma autonomy for DARPA and the military needs exactly this kind of validated testing.
Bear case
  • Evidence not yet accepted. No device submission has publicly used Inner Logic's evidence package.
  • Better-funded rival. adsilico (UK) has raised a £10M Series A for virtual-patient device testing.
  • Free alternative. ARPA-H is paying Kitware to build an open simulation testbed for autonomous stroke robots.
  • Long sales cycles. Device makers move slowly, and many may build simulation in-house as their own IP.
The crux

Will the FDA accept simulation as evidence?

Everything depends on regulators trusting results from virtual patients. The standard for this is credibility assessment of computational models (ASME V&V 40, referenced in FDA guidance). Whoever gets simulation evidence accepted in a real submission first gains a lasting lead.

The dual-use angle

Battlefield autonomy needs a test track

DARPA's surgical competition and MASH program are funding autonomous trauma care, and those systems can't be tested on wounded soldiers. Inner Logic's first proof point was orthopedic trauma. That makes it a natural testing partner for defense programs, which is where J2's government relationships could help.

Where simulation and data in medtech are heading

What is already happening, and what I expect next.

Already happening

Regulators are building the rulebook

The FDA issued final guidance on computational modeling in November 202312. It has qualified 21 medical device development tools, nine of them non-clinical assessment models such as virtual MRI-safety evaluations (2021) and a virtual screw pullout test (2025)13.

Already happening

Animal testing is being phased down

The FDA's April 2025 roadmap set timeframes to phase out animal testing where equivalent alternatives exist, including computational models. In its first year it qualified its first AI-based drug development tool14. The roadmap covers drugs, and device policy often follows.

Already happening

Data is becoming the moat

Surgical video libraries now run past 30,000 hours15, and robots like SRT-H learn procedures from them. Whoever holds the most realistic patient and procedure data builds the most credible simulations.

My view

From design tool to standard evidence

I expect simulation to move from design support to a standard part of submissions for procedural devices within the decade, starting where the FDA already accepts it. Autonomous devices will speed this up, because their edge cases can't all be tested physically.

My view

Defense first, then consolidation

Battlefield autonomy will be validated first, because DARPA and the Defense Health Agency are already paying for it. Over time, I expect the large simulation and device companies to buy the validation specialists, which gives J2 a clear exit path.

06 · Where J2 could invest next

Build out the dual-use validation layer, and back the companies that need it

In my search, I found no other U.S. startup doing in silico testing for procedural devices; the closest rivals are in the UK and Germany. J2 already has a stake in the leading U.S. company in medical simulation training and validation, Inner Logic, and the rest of the layer is still early. The companies building it lack dual-use pull today because they don't have defense customers yet, which is exactly what J2's network can add. The autonomous-device makers come second: they create the demand and are natural Inner Logic customers. See the full tracker.
